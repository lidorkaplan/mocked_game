Design Memo – the logic behind SKYLINE

Mock FRC game · published as the theme edition Steampunk SKYFORGE · 2026-09-23 · Companion to the Steampunk SKYFORGE manual (PDF) and 3D field
In one paragraph. SKYLINE is a game about height as a design choice. Every decision was tuned so that no single robot architecture does everything well. A short robot (≤ 32 in.) owns the space under the floating boards, the low goal and a cheap endgame. A tall robot owns the high goals and the hang. A shooter owns the vertical goal. The best alliance has to combine them. The field adds a second axis, near vs far: cheap goals sit near home, while the valuable goals and the vertical-goal RP sit in the opponent's half, behind an opponent-owned blocker.
  • Core trade-off: go under or go tall (32 in. board clearance between GOAL 1 and GOAL 2 placement heights).
  • Core tension: safe and slow near home vs valuable and contested far away.
  • RPs reward breadth (all three levels), shooting (vertical goal) and mixed endgames (1 HANG + 1 UNDER is enough).

Two principles guided every choice: don't add rules the game doesn't need (inherit 2025 by default), and make each capability cost something, so that choosing a robot architecture is a real strategic decision.

2 What the game rewards (theme of play)

The name SKYLINE describes the silhouette: goals of three heights along one wall, a vertical goal raised above the alliance wall, and boards floating above the carpet. The game's incentives follow the same vertical logic.

2.1 The height ladder – every number is a decision boundary

  • 32 in. board clearance is the keystone. A robot that places into GOAL 1 can fit under (cube top ≈ 27 in.). A robot that places into GOAL 2 cannot (≈ 47 in.) unless it telescopes, which costs mechanism complexity.
  • 78 in. max height implements your rule literally: goal 3 rim plus two cube heights. A lift can get a cube over GOAL 3, but can't tower over the field with a stack.
  • 48 in. "duck" limit in the opponent's launch zone keeps defenders from swatting shots, and it has a side effect: your own GOAL 1 sits in the opponent's launch zone, so even a tall robot has to duck to score GOAL 1. That quietly favours short robots on the low goal.

The point ladder (2 / 3 / 5 / 4) pays for height and distance. The vertical goal is worth slightly less than GOAL 3 because it's a bigger target (30 in. hexagon vs 11 in. throat). Its extra incentive is the SKYLINE/SKYFORGE RP, not raw points.

2.3 Precision, not just size

The throat is 11 in. for a 9 in. cube: about 1 in. clearance per side and roughly ±15° yaw tolerance. A cube turned 45° simply doesn't fit. This rewards controlled, square placement and makes shooting into the horizontal goals possible but risky, as your brief asked ("slightly larger than the cube, you can shoot them in"). The vertical goal is the opposite: a large opening, but shooting is mandatory.

2.4 Risk vs safety for human players

Each alliance has three introduction points. IP-A and IP-B feed into the protected SAFE ZONE, which is safe but at the far end of your own field. IP-C feeds the open field closer to mid-field: faster, but opponents may take those cubes. The human player chooses where supply appears, which is a small but real strategic lever. No introduction during AUTO, as specified.

3.3 Where the interesting interactions happen

  • The launch zone: shooters from one alliance and low-goal scorers from the other share the same 90 × 72 in. space. Neither side is protected there.
  • IP-C: an unprotected supply point that invites a quick steal.
  • Centre line in AUTO: 28 cubes, first come first served.
  • Under the boards: 8 cubes that tall robots can't reach well; a quiet reward for building short.
  • GOAL 3 in the opponent's half: the richest goal is the one you have to drive through traffic to reach.

6 Independent review and what it changed

A GPT-6 reviewer audited the manual for broken or missing items and loopholes. Four high-severity findings were fixed, and a second pass confirmed the fixes introduced nothing new:

  1. Adjacent placement into the vertical goal: a robot could reach around its opponent's GOAL 1. Added a 24 in. release line and a requirement that the cube is released behind it and travels unsupported.
  2. Reciprocal RP farming: both alliances could legally feed each other's vertical goals near their own walls. Now only the owner's legal deliveries score or count toward the RP.
  3. Rule conflict: an inherited 2025 card example ("climbing on the BARGE") contradicted legal board climbing. G211 was modified.
  4. Impossible geometry: GOAL 1 was specified to discharge through a wall 150 in. away. It now discharges through the adjacent alliance wall.

7 The branding theme – Steampunk SKYFORGE

AspectLogic
Why a theme at allFun and identity. A named, branded game is easier to talk about, present and rally a team around. It's a skin only: same field, rules, points and geometry (checked by a content comparison and a STEP geometry re-import).
Why "Steampunk"Homage to FRC Team 1577 Steampunk, which you pointed to. It's a fan tribute, not affiliated with or endorsed by the team, and it doesn't reuse their mark.
Why "SKYFORGE"Sky: floating boards, high goals, the vertical goal above the wall; it keeps the spirit of SKYLINE. Forge: steam, brass and making things, i.e. what building a robot is. One word, easy to say, and it fits your "Steampunk <name>" pattern.
Logo symbolismA brass cog (the machine, and the steampunk genre), an ember-orange hexagon (the vertical goal, with orange as a nod to 1577's avatar colour), a yellow cube (the game piece), and a steam wisp (the forge).
PaletteSoot black, brass, ember orange, parchment. Alliance red and blue and the cube yellow are unchanged, so the field still reads correctly.
SeparationThe themed files were generated from the un-themed SKYLINE working files, which aren't part of this repository. Rules, field and scoring are identical; only the name, colours and logo differ.

8 Open questions – what to playtest first

  1. Cycle simulation to tune the RP thresholds (5 / 8 / 5 / 10) and the point ladder, so the 2-point GOAL 1 is still worth a robot's time.
  2. Throat prototype with real foam cubes: does ±15° hold, and can cubes be shot into an 11 in. throat at all?
  3. Board clearance sweep (30–34 in.): is 32 in. the right split between low and tall robots?
  4. Launch-zone congestion: is the shared zone fun, or does it become a pushing match?
  5. Human-player throughput vs 26 reserve cubes per alliance.
  6. Board structure: the cantilevered 600 lb support hasn't been engineered.
